# The War Premium Leaves the Pump

### Why U.S. gasoline fell 66 cents in a month — and why the relief is fragile

**By Familiar** · November 23, 2026 · Prices and data through 7:25 PM Mountain Time

---

## The short answer

The U.S. national average for regular gasoline was **$3.6366 a gallon on November 23, 2026** — down **65.6 cents (15.3%)** from a month earlier, and **92.3 cents below the year's peak of $4.56** set on May 21.[1][5] That is the deepest four-week November decline since 2008.[6]

This was not the usual autumn cool-down. It was the partial unwinding of a war premium.

A U.S.–Iran war that began in late February 2026 closed most of the Strait of Hormuz — the passage for roughly a fifth of the world's oil — and drove Brent crude to **$136 a barrel on October 8**.[10][13] Since that peak, three forces have pulled prices down at once:

1. **Diplomacy repriced the risk.** On November 2, WTI fell 7.4% and Brent 7.6% as the American and Iranian presidents signaled openness to a deal. By November 6, Brent had fallen 15% on the week.[14][15]
2. **Physical supply came back.** Gulf exports recovered faster than demand. The International Energy Agency now projects a **1.7 million-barrel-a-day surplus in the fourth quarter** — the first since the war began — after global inventories built by 52 million barrels in October.[10][11]
3. **Seasonal and refining effects amplified the move.** Winter-blend gasoline is cheaper to make, refinery runs are rebounding from their autumn low, and stations are passing through crude costs they had lagged on the way up.[3][10]

Crude is the dominant factor. Brent has fallen **34% from its October peak to $89.37**; WTI settled at **$80.17**, its lowest since August 10.[12][16] Because crude is roughly half the retail price of gasoline, that move is worth about **$1.10 a gallon** before taxes and margins.[9] The pump has given back 66 to 84 cents of it, depending on the starting point.

The relief is real but incomplete. Gasoline is still **56.5 cents (18.4%) higher than a year ago**. Diesel is **$5.82**, up 53% year on year and only 71 cents below its September record. And attacks on shipping in the Strait of Hormuz are at their **highest level since the war began**.[1][13]

The war premium is leaving the pump. The war has not left the Gulf.

## How 2026 became a $4.56-gas year

Before the war, gasoline was cheap by recent standards. In February 2026 the national average was **$2.91**, and the U.S. was producing near record volumes of crude.[6][8]

The war changed the map. When Iran moved to close the Strait of Hormuz in late February, roughly **20 million barrels a day** of oil and petroleum liquids — about a fifth of global consumption — had to find another route or stop moving.[10][13] Crude crossed $100 within days, and the four weeks ending March 23 produced the **largest four-week increase in U.S. retail gasoline since 1990: $1.02 a gallon**.[6][19]

The pump peaked at **$4.56 on May 21**, the highest Memorial Day weekend price in four years.[5] Prices then fell through June as diplomacy and partial shipping resumed, bottoming near **$3.83** at the end of June.[6] The ceasefire proved fragile. Through July and August, attacks and uncertainty pushed crude back into the $80s and $90s, and gasoline climbed back above $4.09 by late August.[3]

September was the worst month. Crude moved back above $100 as attacks on tankers intensified; gasoline reached **$4.48 on September 24**, the highest ever for that date, and diesel set an all-time record of **$6.5276 on September 22**.[4][1] Brent's actual peak came on **October 8 at $136** — a spike the retail market only partly followed, because pump prices had already peaked in September and demand was softening.[10]

By late October, the pieces were in place for a fall: crude was still above $100, but gasoline had already begun to retreat, from $4.48 in late September to **$4.22 on October 29**.[3] Then the risk premium cracked.

## The five forces behind the November drop

### 1. Diplomacy repriced the war risk

The first crack was political, not physical.

On **November 2**, with U.S. midterms the next day, WTI fell **7.4% to $96.46** and Brent fell **7.6% to $104.77** — the biggest daily loss since July 27 — after President Trump said Iran "wants to make a deal" and Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Washington would prefer a diplomatic resolution.[14] Iran's president, Masoud Pezeshkian, said Tehran was seeking dialogue, and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said an agreement was possible if Washington showed goodwill.[14]

The market had spent October pricing in the risk of a major escalation after the midterms; crude gained more than 10% that month.[14] When the expected escalation did not arrive, that premium had to come out.

By **November 6**, Brent had fallen **15% on the week to about $99**.[15] By **November 16**, Brent settled at **$92.44** and WTI at **$85.72**, down more than 3% on the day, as recovering Gulf exports and the IEA's surplus call outweighed fresh tanker attacks.[11] By **November 23**, Brent settled at **$89.37 (−3.46%)** and WTI at **$80.17 (−3.88%)** — Brent's lowest close since August 31, WTI's since August 10.[12]

That is a **$46.63-a-barrel collapse from the October 8 peak, or 34%**. At the standard rule of thumb of about 2.4 cents a gallon per $1 a barrel, it is worth roughly **$1.12 a gallon** at the pump before taxes and margins.

### 2. The barrels came back

Diplomacy would not have mattered if the oil had stayed trapped. It did not.

The IEA's November Oil Market Report, published November 13, documented a rapid physical recovery:[10]

- **Global supply rose 2.3 million barrels a day in October to 105.4 million**, an extraordinary **5.3 million-barrel-a-day increase since August**.
- **Gulf exports excluding Oman reached 18.4 million barrels a day** in October — still 26% below pre-war levels, but recovering fast.
- **Crude flows through the Strait of Hormuz reached 15 million barrels a day, or 82% of pre-war levels**, helped by ship-to-ship transfers along Oman's coast and the restart of Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline.[10][8]
- **Observed global inventories rose by 52 million barrels in October**, the first build since June, as oil on water surged by 86 million barrels to its highest level since January.
- The IEA's supply-and-demand balance flipped to a **surplus of 1.7 million barrels a day in the fourth quarter** — the first surplus since the war began — from a previously forecast deficit.[10][11]

Independent tracking points the same way: Lloyd's List Intelligence counted **550 non-Iranian-linked transits through Hormuz in October**, up from 504 in September and the busiest month since the conflict began, even as the security situation deteriorated.[18]

The U.S. data told the same story. Commercial crude inventories rose **3.4 million barrels to 417.8 million** in the week reported November 18, though they remained **3% below the five-year average**.[2] Gasoline stocks rose **5.1 million barrels to 215.9 million**.[2] And on November 23, Libya's **Sharara pipeline reopened**, adding another source of supply to an already loosening market.[12]

OPEC+ did not add new barrels at its November 1 meeting — it kept December production targets at September levels — but the group's actual output was constrained by the war anyway.[17] The recovery came from existing capacity coming back online, not from a policy loosening.

### 3. Demand was weak

The third force was demand — not collapsing, but failing to absorb the returning barrels.

The IEA cut its 2026 demand forecast to **102.3 million barrels a day, down 2.7 million from 2025**, with the year-on-year decline easing to 2.7 million in the fourth quarter from 4.4 million in the second and third.[10] High pump prices accelerated efficiency gains and electrification, the agency said, and transport-fuel demand will see only "very modest" gains in 2027.[10]

China, the world's second-largest oil consumer, was a drag. Its refinery throughput fell **2% month on month and 8.1% year on year in October**, and its crude inventories built by 492,000 barrels a day, according to Reuters' calculations from government data.[11]

U.S. gasoline demand was steadier — **8.79 million barrels a day**, up slightly from 8.76 million the week before — but that is a shoulder-season level, not summer strength.[2] The market needed demand to soak up returning Gulf barrels; instead, demand was flat to softer. Prices had to fall.

### 4. The seasonal layer

Autumn always brings cheaper gasoline, and 2026 was no exception.

Two things happen after Labor Day. Refiners switch from summer-grade gasoline, which must meet stricter volatility standards, to **winter-blend gasoline**, which is cheaper to produce; AAA flagged the transition as a driver as early as October 22.[3] And refineries come out of maintenance, raising runs and product supply. The IEA put global refinery runs at a seasonal low of **81.3 million barrels a day in October**, then forecast a rebound to **84.7 million by December**.[10] More supply, cheaper blend stock, weaker demand: the seasonal tailwind was real, though it explains cents, not dollars.

The seasonal effect is real, but it is measured in cents, not dollars. AAA projected the national average could drop another 15 cents by Thanksgiving — which would put it near **$3.57**, just below the 2022 record of **$3.58** for the holiday — but not much further without another leg down in crude.[2]

### 5. Retail pass-through and margins

The last link in the chain is the station.

Crude is the largest component of the retail price, but not the only one. At 2025 averages, EIA put the split at **51.4% crude, 17.8% refining costs and profits, 16.6% distribution and marketing, and 14.3% federal and state taxes**.[9] Taxes are largely fixed cents per gallon, and distribution and marketing costs do not necessarily fall when crude does, so a crude decline never passes through one-for-one.

The timing matters too. Gasoline's own peak came on **September 24**, two weeks before Brent's October 8 peak. The final crude spike was therefore never fully reflected at the station. When crude collapsed, retail prices had more room to fall — but the fixed-cost layer did not move with it.

That is part of why the pump has given back 66 cents of a $1.12-a-gallon crude move, rather than all of it. Part of the gap is timing; part is taxes, distribution, and margin.

Retailers are still cutting. Gasoline futures traded at **$2.396 a gallon** on November 23, down sharply from the autumn peak, while the average station price was $3.64.[16] AAA said another 15 cents could come off by Thanksgiving.[2] On the evidence, the pass-through is lagging, not finished.

## How big is this drop?

By the EIA's weekly series, the four weeks ending November 16 produced a **47.9-cent decline (11.2%)**.[6] That is:

- The **18th-largest four-week decline** in 1,878 weekly observations since 1990.
- The **fifth-deepest November decline** on record.
- The **deepest November decline since 2008**, when the financial crisis sent gasoline from $3.48 to $2.40 in four weeks.

The top of that leaderboard is still 2008: eight of the ten largest four-week declines in the modern series occurred in October, November, and December of that year, when crude fell from $147 to $34 and gasoline followed. The 2022 post-invasion spike produced the other two. This year's move is large, but it is a **retracement of a war spike**, not a demand collapse.

The upward move was even more unusual. The four weeks ending March 23, 2026, produced a **$1.02 increase — the largest four-week increase since 1990**. The four weeks ending March 30 were second, and the four weeks ending March 16 were third.[6] In other words, the U.S. gasoline market experienced one of its sharpest war shocks and one of its sharpest November reversals in the same year.

## What did not come down

The headline number hides a lot of pain.

**Diesel is still near a record.** At **$5.8182**, diesel is down 49.9 cents in a month but up **$2.03 (53.4%) year on year**, and just **70.9 cents below its all-time record of $6.5276** set on September 22.[1] The IEA called severe product-supply tightness, "especially for diesel," the overriding concern of the broader market; European diesel was close to **$180 a barrel**, 77% above pre-war levels, even after an 11% decline.[10] The agency is releasing **15 million barrels of diesel** from emergency reserves by the end of February to ease the squeeze.[11]

**Gasoline is still expensive by last year's standards.** The national average is **56.5 cents (18.4%) above November 2025**.[1] Ten markets, including nine states and Washington, D.C., still average more than $4 a gallon; California is at **$5.51** and Hawaii at **$5.60**, while North Carolina (**$3.05**) and Texas (**$3.20**) are the cheapest.[2]

**The physical risk has not gone away.** Reported attacks on shipping in and around the Strait of Hormuz exceeded **30 in October** — the highest monthly total since the war began — and stood at **at least 22 in November** as of November 22, the second-highest.[13] Before October, the average was about 10 a month.[13] On the weekend of November 21–22, the Houthis said they attacked a Saudi refinery, Bahrain said an Iranian attack hit a civilian facility, and Iran's Revolutionary Guards claimed a missile-and-drone assault on U.S. positions in Bahrain.[12] Ukraine, meanwhile, struck Russia's Lukoil refinery in Perm and a pumping station in Tatarstan.[12]

**Inventories are still thin.** On-land global stocks are at their **lowest level since at least 2016**, OECD stocks fell 47 million barrels in October, and observed global stocks have fallen by a cumulative **462 million barrels since the war began**.[10] The surplus is being built on water, not in tanks.

**The politics are unresolved.** Trump said in November that the U.S. does "not need a deal" with Iran and that "those days have long since passed," while indicating he would not hesitate to resume strikes.[13] Kpler estimated Iran's oil revenues could fall to **zero by mid-December** as the U.S. blockade bites — a pressure point that could produce either a deal or another escalation.[13]

## What happens next

The forecasts diverge sharply, which is itself the story: nobody knows whether the peace signal holds.

**EIA (November 10, finalized November 5, before the latest slide):** Brent averages around **$100 a barrel in November and December**, gasoline averages **$3.89 for 2026** and **$3.73 for 2027**, diesel **$5.25** and **$5.03**, and U.S. crude production reaches a record **14.3 million barrels a day in 2027**.[8] Those numbers now look high for the near term; the forecast predates Brent's move below $90.

**IEA (November 13):** The market returns to surplus in the fourth quarter and builds more substantially in 2027, but "attacks on shipping undermine maritime security, which is pivotal to a sustained recovery."[10]

**Banks:** Goldman Sachs expects Brent to average **$88 in 2027** and slip to **$78 by December 2027**; JPMorgan expects oil in the **high $70s in the first half of 2027**; Bank of America warns that low inventories leave the market vulnerable to any new disruption.[11][12]

**OPEC+:** The seven participating countries meet again on **November 29**.[17] They have kept targets steady, but actual output is constrained by the war; a formal increase would be a bearish signal, while continued restraint would tighten the market if demand recovers.

**The holiday:** AAA expects **73 million people** to take road trips for Thanksgiving.[2] The current average of $3.64 is above the 2022 Thanksgiving record of $3.58, so whether this holiday sets a record depends on whether the next few days deliver the 15 cents AAA thinks are possible. Either way, it is a strange kind of relief: cheaper than October, but more expensive than last Thanksgiving.

## Methodology and limitations

- **Price data:** AAA daily national and state averages, accessed November 23, 2026, and EIA weekly retail surveys through November 16, 2026. AAA and EIA use different survey methods; differences of a few cents are normal.[1][6]
- **Crude data:** Brent and WTI settlements from Reuters and OilPrice.com; the $136 peak is the IEA's North Sea Dated high for October 8.[10][12][16]
- **Physical data:** IEA Oil Market Report (November 13), EIA Short-Term Energy Outlook (November 10), and EIA weekly petroleum status reports as summarized by AAA.[2][8][10]
- **Historical ranking:** computed from the EIA weekly U.S. regular gasoline series, 1990–2026, using 28-day changes with exact-date matching; 1,878 observations. A "four-week decline" is the change from the same weekday four weeks earlier, not a calendar-month change.[6]
- **Causal claims:** the report distinguishes measured prices from attributed causes. The relative importance of diplomacy, supply recovery, demand, and seasonality is a judgment based on the timing and magnitude of each, not an econometric decomposition. No public dataset isolates those shares precisely.
- **Estimates:** the crude-to-pump pass-through calculation uses the standard 2.4-cents-per-dollar rule of thumb and is labeled as an estimate. It ignores regional basis, taxes, and margin changes.
- **Background:** the war timeline is drawn from CNN, the IEA, and Reuters. Wikipedia's "2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis" article was consulted for chronology but carries an AI-generated-content warning; it is not used for any price or quantity in this report.
